asym <- function(s, var, drop_crash) {
  d <- eq |> filter(symbol == s) |> inner_join(mk, by = c("market", "month")) |> inner_join(rf, by = c("market", "month")) |>
    inner_join(o_log, by = "month") |> filter(month >= as.Date("2011-02-01")) |>
    mutate(ex = ret - rf, mex = mkt - rf, x = .data[[var]], up = pmax(x, 0), dn = pmin(x, 0))
  if (drop_crash) d <- d |> filter(!month %in% as.Date(c("2020-03-01", "2020-04-01")))
  m <- lm(ex ~ mex + up + dn, d); V <- nw(m); ca <- matrix(c(0, 0, 1, -1), 1)

    p = 2 * pnorm(-abs(drop(ca %*% coef(m)) / sqrt(drop(ca %*% V %*% t(ca))))))
}

TipAnswer first
  • Australia’s fuel supply now runs through Asian refineries. Domestic refinery throughput halved after the 2021 closures (2428 → 1239 megalitres a month, 2015–19 vs 2023–26), and refined-product imports rose 50%. In 2025, 96% of those imports came from Asian refiners: Singapore (26%), South Korea (25%), Malaysia (13%) and others. Stock cover has averaged 60 IEA days, against a 90-day obligation met in only 13 of 194 months.
  • Asia’s crude benchmark broke away from Brent in 2026, but Singapore’s import bill did not. The Brent–Dubai spread, normally 1.9 dollars a barrel, reached 27.7 dollars in April 2026. Over 2011–26, Singapore’s actual crude import prices (SingStat) track Dubai most closely, yet in the 2026 shock they rose roughly in line with Brent, far more than Dubai (up 88% by September 2026). Asian buyers paid for the spike regardless of benchmark.
  • APAC energy stocks are exposed to oil, in the expected order. Monthly oil betas (controlling for each home market) run from 0.34 for upstream producers down to 0.04 for refining-heavy Sinopec. Whether they react more to oil rises or falls is not robust. The answer flips with how returns are measured and with a few extreme months.
  • Regional fundamentals add little beyond the price. Singapore bunker demand, Australian stock cover and the Brent–Dubai spread neither predict oil returns (joint p = 0.62) nor explain energy-stock returns once Brent is controlled for (no stock significant after a multiple-testing correction).
  • Oil exposure is not rewarded. Across all 51 stocks, the Fama–MacBeth price of oil risk is -0.42% a month (Shanken t = -0.25). So what: in APAC equities, oil is a risk to hedge or diversify, not a source of expected return. Investors are not paid for holding it.

Question

Oil matters more to Asia-Pacific than to almost any other region. China, Japan, Korea and India are the world’s largest crude importers, Singapore is the region’s refining and trading hub, and Australia has become dependent on imported fuel. This module asks four questions:

  1. How has the region’s oil supply chain changed, using Australia’s official petroleum statistics as a lens?
  2. Do regional fundamentals (Singapore marine-fuel demand, Australian stock levels, Asia’s Dubai benchmark) carry information about oil prices?
  3. How exposed are APAC energy stocks to oil, and does that exposure differ between producers and refiners, or between rising and falling prices?
  4. Is oil risk priced? Do stocks with more oil exposure earn higher average returns?

The two oil price sources were compared before use. Like-for-like monthly averages from FRED and the World Bank agree to within about 50 cents on average. Month-end prices (FRED) are used for returns, because they line up in time with month-end stock returns; averaged prices are smoother and partly lagged. Australia’s imports by source were checked against the national total: the country rows sum to the total to within 0.05% in every month. Weekly Singapore onshore oil inventories, the region’s most-watched stock data, are published by Enterprise Singapore but were not available in a downloadable form. EMA’s annual stock changes and the monthly bunker sales are used instead. The EMA workbook is read from a local copy and is not redistributed with this project.

  theme_report()

The decline came in two waves. Throughput averaged about 3500 megalitres a month in 2011, fell to about 2400 after earlier closures, then to about 1200 after the 2021 closures. Each time, imports filled the gap. Stock cover has sat well below the International Energy Agency’s 90-day obligation for most of the period. It was 52 days in August 2026. That is about 7 weeks of net imports as a buffer against a regional supply disruption.

Dubai crude, the Middle East grade that supplies most of Asia, normally trades a little below Brent: the spread averaged 1.9 dollars a barrel (standard deviation 1.6) before 2026. In 2026 it blew out, reaching 27.7 dollars in April 2026. On benchmark prices alone, Asian buyers of Middle East crude would seem to have escaped much of the shock. Singapore’s actual import prices, below, show they did not.

Singapore: Asia’s oil hub

Singapore refines imported crude and re-exports fuel across Asia and to Australia, and it is the world’s largest marine-fuel (bunkering) port. Three Singapore sources are used here: the Energy Market Authority’s annual Singapore Energy Statistics (imports, exports, stock changes and refinery flows, 2005–2025), the Maritime and Port Authority’s monthly bunker sales, and SingStat’s monthly import price indices for crude oil and refined products.

  • The hub has shrunk since its peak. Singapore’s petroleum-product exports peaked at 101.3 million tonnes of oil equivalent in 2017, fell 29% to 2022, and have partly recovered (81.4 million in 2025). Refinery input went from 47.1 million in 2009 to 43.7 million in 2024. This matches Australia’s falling Singapore share above: Australia now buys more of its fuel from refiners in Korea, Malaysia, Taiwan, China and India.
  • Bunker demand moves with fuel-oil supply year to year. Annual growth in MPA bunker sales correlates 0.58 with growth in fuel-oil imports plus heavy refinery output (2010–2024). The long-run trends differ: bunker sales kept rising while fuel-oil supply fell, plausibly as ships switched to lower-sulphur blends after the 2020 IMO rules.
  • Stock changes carry no price signal. Singapore’s annual oil stock changes are uncorrelated with the change in Brent (r = 0.21, p = 0.47, 14 years), in line with the Australian result below.

What Singapore actually pays. SingStat’s import price indices measure the landed price of what Singapore imports. Converted to US dollars, they give a direct test of which benchmark describes Asia’s oil costs.

Both benchmarks track Singapore’s import costs closely, and Dubai slightly more so. When both are included in one regression, Dubai carries the weight (coefficient 0.77, t = 4.6; Brent 0.18, t = 1.3; R² = 0.86). That fits Singapore’s reliance on Middle East crude, although the two benchmarks are too correlated for the difference to be statistically decisive (p = 0.06 for equal coefficients).

The 2026 shock breaks the long-run pattern. Singapore’s crude import prices rose far more than Dubai and roughly in line with Brent, peaking 88% above December 2025 in September 2026. They were above Dubai in 7 of 9 months of 2026 and above Brent in 5. Refined-product import prices rose most of all, peaking 105% above December 2025 in April 2026. Across 2026 they stayed closer to Brent (average gap 10.5 index points) than to Dubai (19.3). The data cannot say why. Two plausible reasons are that the index is measured on a landed basis, so it includes freight and insurance costs that typically jump in a supply shock, and that refiners may have switched to Atlantic-basin crude priced off Brent. Either way, the cheaper Dubai benchmark did not translate into cheaper oil for Asia’s main hub.

  • Producers are most exposed. A 10% rise in Brent goes with roughly a 2.4%–3.4% excess return for Woodside, Santos, Beach and CNOOC, after controlling for the home market. Integrated PetroChina is in between, and Sinopec, mostly a refiner and fuel retailer, has almost no exposure (0.04, t = 0.8).
  • The Australian refiners are exposed too. Ampol (0.11) and Viva Energy (0.30) have positive oil betas. Plausibly, higher crude prices come with higher product prices and inventory gains for fuel distributors. Either way, they are not a hedge against oil.
  • Regional fundamentals add nothing systematic. Only 1 stock has a joint p-value below 5%, and none does after adjusting for testing eight stocks (smallest adjusted p = 0.31).

Do stocks react more to oil rises or falls? Not robustly.

Show code 
asym_sum |> transmute(Specification = spec, `Rising-oil beta larger` = paste(n_up_bigger, "of", nrow(eres)),
                      `Significant difference (BH 5%)` = n_sig) |> kable(align = "lrr")

The answer depends on choices that should not matter. With simple returns over the full sample, falling oil appears to hit producers harder. With log returns, the rising-oil beta is larger for most stocks, but never significantly so. Dropping March and April 2020 changes it again. The reason is that a few extreme months dominate a 15-year sample: Brent moved +89% (May 2020), +78% (Mar 2026), -71% (Mar 2020). The overall oil betas are stable: switching to log returns moves no stock’s beta by more than 0.03. Only the split into rising and falling months is fragile, so no conclusion about asymmetry is drawn.

Is oil risk priced?

Show code 
bind_rows(fm2 |> mutate(Model = "Market + oil"), fm4 |> mutate(Model = "Market + oil + iron ore + AUD")) |>
  transmute(Model, Factor = recode(term, gamma0 = "Intercept", MKT = "Market (regional)", oil = "Oil (Brent)",
                                   iron = "Iron ore", aud = "AUD/USD"),
            `Premium (monthly)` = pct(estimate, 2), `t (Shanken)` = num(t_shanken)) |>
  kable(align = "llrr")

Stocks differ in their oil exposure (betas range from -0.14 to 0.39 across the universe), but higher exposure has not come with higher average returns. The estimated oil premium is -0.42% a month, with a Shanken t-statistic of -0.25. Iron ore and the Australian dollar, Australia’s other big commodity exposures, are not priced either. The test has limited power: the standard error on the oil premium is about 1.7% a month, so a modest premium cannot be ruled out. But there is no evidence that APAC investors are paid for bearing commodity risk, consistent with Chen, Roll and Ross’s (1986) finding for oil in US data. The market factor and the Australian dollar are highly correlated in USD returns (0.81), which makes their separate premia hard to distinguish.

Limitations

  • One country’s fundamentals. Australia’s petroleum statistics are unusually detailed and free, but Australia is a price-taker. Chinese, Japanese and Korean refinery runs and stocks would be more informative about regional demand and are not freely available in this form.
  • No refining margins. Singapore’s benchmark refining margins and product cracks are commercial data. The refiners’ oil betas therefore mix the effects of crude prices and margins, which often move together.
  • Monthly data, global price. Monthly returns are too coarse to capture intra-month timing, and the Brent close (London) and Asian closes do not overlap. Weekly returns would address this at the cost of noisier fundamentals.
  • Singapore data are annual or price-based. EMA’s energy statistics are annual (14–21 observations), and SingStat’s import prices are indices, not volumes, so the Singapore tests have little statistical power.
  • Viva Energy has only 97 months of history (listed 2018).
  • Survivor sample and low power in the pricing test, as in Modules 03 and 04.
